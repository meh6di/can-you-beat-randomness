\begin{abstract}
We ask whether statistical models can extract a profit from the pricing of the five major European football leagues, and, more importantly, how much evidence a realistic sample can provide either way. Models (tuned logistic regression, a market--strength log-linear blend, raw and calibrated XGBoost) are retrained every month in a walk-forward design on Elo and exponentially weighted form features that carry over seasons. The betting rule and threshold are chosen on one development season by closing-line value (CLV) against Pinnacle and reported once on an untouched holdout season. Every ROI is accompanied by a bootstrap interval and by a p-value against an efficient-market null in which outcomes are redrawn from de-vigged market probabilities; a power analysis states the smallest edge the sample could detect. Probabilistic quality is assessed with log loss, Brier score and the ranked probability score, and calibration with ECE against its sampling-noise floor. A separate study of the market itself examines favourite--longshot bias, and bankroll risk is quantified by simulation under several staking schemes. No model or betting strategy demonstrates statistically significant outperformance of the market: all holdout ROIs are consistent with the efficient-market null, the optimised blend weight collapses to $w\approx0$, and the smallest edge the holdout could detect is 22--33\% ROI for the 6\% threshold strategies (139--226 bets on the full holdout). The logistic regression shows a small, statistically significant pick skill ($+2.2\%$), but its closing-line value remains negative and its holdout ROI is indistinguishable from luck. For educational purposes only; not betting advice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Betting advertising is ubiquitous around football. The question is whether algorithmic models can exploit inefficiencies in the odds, or whether the bookmaker's margin guarantees long-run losses. A tempting but weak way to answer it is to report one season's ROI. With a per-bet standard deviation of roughly 1.3--1.6 units, a sample of a few hundred bets has a standard error of roughly 9--13 percentage points of ROI (Section~\ref{sec:betting}), far larger than any plausible edge. This report therefore centres on \emph{inference}: intervals, nulls, power, and a low-variance edge measure (CLV), and only then on profit.

\paragraph{Disclaimer.} Solely for educational, academic and research purposes. Not financial or gambling advice.

\section{Data}
Data come from \url{football-data.co.uk} (\texttt{fetch\_data.py}): Premier League, La Liga, Serie A, Bundesliga and Ligue~1, seasons 2019--20 to 2025--26. The 2020--21 season (mostly played without crowds) is used only to build ratings and form, never to train or test. \texttt{clean\_data.py} validates downloads and rows (decimal odds must exceed 1, duplicates are dropped) and keeps results, goals, shots on target, corners, average, best and Pinnacle odds, and Pinnacle \emph{closing} odds. Closing odds are missing for part of the holdout; this matters for which matches each analysis uses (see the start of Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Features and Leakage Control}
Features are built per league across seasons (\texttt{engineer\_features.py}); the value at a match uses only strictly earlier days.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{De-vigged market probabilities.} With overround $\Omega=\sum_k 1/o_k$, proportional de-vigging gives $p_k=(1/o_k)/\Omega$. We use the \emph{power} method, $p_k=(1/o_k)^{c}$ with $c\ge1$ such that $\sum_k p_k=1$, which removes proportionally more margin from longshots. The model sees the log-ratios $\log(p_H/p_A)$ and $\log\big(p_D/(p_H+p_A)\big)$, which avoids collinearity.
  \item \textbf{Elo.} Home advantage, a goal-difference multiplier, regression towards the mean between seasons, and lower starting ratings for promoted teams.
  \item \textbf{Exponentially weighted form for both teams:} points, goals for (attack) and against (defence), shots on target for/against, corners for/against, with half-life of five matches, shrunk to the league mean while a team has few games ($n/(n+3)$), plus games-played and rest days.
\end{itemize}
A unit test perturbs the result of one match and verifies that no feature at or before that match changes.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Walk-forward protocol}
For every calendar month $M$ of the 2024--25 and 2025--26 seasons: training data are matches before $M-365$ days; the validation window is the 365 days before $M$ and tunes regularisation, the blend weight and the calibrator; month $M$ is the test set. Models are therefore refit as results arrive and no information after $M$ is used. 2024--25 is the \emph{development} season; 2025--26 is the \emph{holdout}.

\subsection{Models}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Logistic regression:} standardised features, league dummies, $C$ tuned on validation.
  \item \textbf{Blend:} $p\propto p_{\text{mkt}}^{1-w}\,p_{\text{strength}}^{w}$, where $p_{\text{strength}}$ comes from a model without market inputs and $w\in[0,1]$ is fitted on validation. $w\approx0$ means the model adds nothing beyond the market.
  \item \textbf{XGBoost}, raw and recalibrated on the later validation window with a multinomial vector-scaling map.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Betting rules}
One unit is staked per match on the best outcome if its score clears a threshold: \emph{edge} ($\hat p-p_{\text{mkt}}$) or \emph{expected value} ($\hat p\cdot o-1$), at average, best-available or Pinnacle prices. Rule, threshold and price source are chosen on the development season (maximum mean CLV, at least 150 bets) and evaluated once on the holdout. The reported strategies use thresholds of 3\% and 6\%.

\section{Statistical Framework}
\paragraph{Bootstrap.} Bets are resampled with replacement to give percentile intervals for ROI.
\paragraph{Efficient-market null.} Holding the placed bets fixed, outcomes are redrawn $20{,}000$ times from the de-vigged market probabilities. The resulting ROI distribution is centred near minus the margin, not zero; the p-value is the share of simulations at least as good as the observed ROI.
\paragraph{Power.} With per-bet standard deviation $\sigma$ and $n$ bets, the smallest true ROI detected with 80\% power at 5\% (one-sided) is $(z_{0.95}+z_{0.80})\,\sigma/\sqrt n$, and a target edge $e$ needs $n=\big((z_{0.95}+z_{0.80})\sigma/e\big)^2$ bets.
\paragraph{Closing-line value.} $\mathrm{CLV}=o_{\text{taken}}\,p^{\text{Pinnacle close}}-1$. It does not depend on who won, so its standard error is an order of magnitude smaller than ROI's. ``Pick skill'' subtracts the average CLV of all three outcomes of the same match, isolating whether the model chose the better side from the margin every blind bet pays.
\paragraph{Probabilistic scores.} Log loss, multiclass Brier score and the ranked probability score (outcomes ordered H$<$D$<$A), with paired per-match differences against the market, and expected calibration error (equal-mass bins) compared with the ECE a perfectly calibrated forecaster would show by chance.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Two evaluation samples.} Analyses that need Pinnacle closing odds (closing-line value, pick skill, and the development/holdout strategy selection) drop every match without them (\texttt{clv\_analysis.py} and \texttt{strategy\_select.py}), which leaves 898 holdout matches. All other holdout analyses (ROI and efficient-market null, per-league ROI, paired tests, power, bankroll risk) use every holdout match. The same logistic regression 6\% edge rule therefore places 139 bets on the full holdout but 77 on the closing-odds subset. A bet depends only on its own match, so the 77 are a subset of the 139, and the two ROI figures differ only because of which matches are included.
\subsection{Do the models add information beyond the market?}
\safetable{prob_scores}
\safetable{calibration}
\safefigure{results/reliability.png}{0.95}{Reliability diagrams (equal-mass bins, pooled over H/D/A).}
The market (average odds, de-vigged) achieves the best scores across all metrics (log loss 0.961, Brier 0.571, RPS 0.192). No model beats the market: the calibrated XGBoost is significantly worse ($\Delta$log loss $=+0.010$, 95\% CI $[+0.003, +0.017]$, $p=0.006$). The logistic regression and raw XGBoost are directionally worse, with log-loss and Brier intervals that include zero; the one nominal exception is the raw XGBoost RPS ($p=0.044$, again in the unfavourable direction). Pinnacle de-vigged prices are statistically indistinguishable from the average market. The blend's weight collapses to $w\approx0$, confirming that Elo and form features add no information beyond market prices. Climatology is dramatically worse ($\Delta$log loss $=+0.109$, $p<0.001$), validating the market's informational content. All sources, including the market, are statistically compatible with perfect calibration ($p\ge0.126$ in the ECE table), so calibration does not explain the differences in log loss.

\subsection{Betting outcomes and their uncertainty}\label{sec:betting}
\safetable{stats_overall}
\safetable{stats_per_league}
\safetable{stats_paired}
\safetable{stats_power}
\safefigure{results/market_null.png}{0.95}{Observed ROI against the efficient-market null.}
Across all model--threshold combinations, no strategy achieves statistical significance at the 5\% level. The smallest p-value against the efficient-market null is 0.084 (XGBoost raw, 3\% threshold, ROI $-1.0\%$), which is above even the uncorrected 5\% level; for the 8 strategy variants tested (two of which, the blend, place no bets) a Bonferroni threshold would be $0.05/8\approx0.006$. The highest point-estimate ROI in the overall table is $+0.3\%$ (logistic regression, 6\% threshold), and every bootstrap interval includes zero. ROIs should be compared with the null mean (about $-6\%$ to $-9\%$) rather than with zero: the 3\% logistic regression and raw XGBoost lie 1.1 and 1.4 null standard deviations above it, which is unremarkable. Per-league ROIs rest on 16--62 bets and have intervals tens of points wide; the only interval excluding zero is raw XGBoost in La Liga, on the negative side.

The minimum detectable ROI at 80\% power ranges from 22.2\% (calibrated XGBoost, $n=226$) to 33.2\% (logistic regression, $n=139$) for the 6\% strategies, and is about 45\% for the 77-bet selected strategy on the closing-odds subset (SE of ROI 18.0\%, times $z_{0.95}+z_{0.80}=2.49$). At the 3\% threshold, with roughly 830--860 bets and $\sigma\approx1.5$, the same formula gives about 13\%. All of these are far larger than any plausible betting edge. Detecting a 2\% edge would require 27,700--38,400 bets, which is roughly 30--45 times the bets placed at the 3\% threshold in the holdout.

\subsection{Closing-line value}
\safetable{clv_summary}
\safefigure{results/clv_curves.png}{0.95}{Realised profit versus cumulative CLV.\label{fig:clv}}
The pick-skill analysis isolates directional selection from the overround. For the logistic regression strategy (6\% edge, average odds, 77 holdout bets), pick skill is $+2.18\%$ (95\% CI $[+1.29\%, +3.16\%]$, $p<0.001$): the model chose outcomes whose closing prices were better than the average of all three outcomes of the same match. Raw and calibrated XGBoost show no significant pick skill ($-0.42\%$ and $+1.28\%$; $p=0.559$ and $0.065$). Two caveats apply.

First, a rule that simply backs the favourite already has a CLV of $-4.85\%$ against $-6.88\%$ for a random pick at average odds, a gap of about 2.0 points. Most of the logistic regression's pick skill at average odds is therefore what a lean towards favourites would produce. At best available odds the favourite gap is smaller (about 0.65 points, against $+2.15\%$ for the logistic regression), so some directional skill may remain, but this was not tested separately.

Second, positive pick skill does not make CLV positive. The strategy's mean CLV is $-4.12\%$ at average odds and $-0.46\%$ (95\% CI $[-1.46\%, +0.55\%]$) at best odds, and only 32.5\% of wagers beat the raw closing odds at average prices. A CLV of about $-4\%$ implies an expected ROI near $-4\%$, so the realised ROI of $+7.5\%$ is a lucky outcome of 77 bets (about 0.6 standard errors above that expectation, Figure~\ref{fig:clv}) and not evidence of an edge. The variance ratio (SE of ROI / SE of CLV) is approximately 34:1 for the logistic regression (16--20:1 for the XGBoost strategies), confirming CLV's far greater precision for detecting small edges.

\subsection{Strategy selected on development, evaluated on the holdout}
\safetable{select_holdout}
The strategy selected on the 2024--25 development season (logistic regression, 6\% edge threshold; 161 development bets, 77 holdout bets) behaves differently across price sources. Its mean CLV fell from $-2.5\%$ to $-4.1\%$ at average odds and from $+1.4\%$ to $-0.5\%$ at best odds, but was essentially unchanged at Pinnacle prices ($-1.5\%$ to $-1.3\%$). The rank correlation between development and holdout CLV across the parameter grid is $\rho=0.74$ ($0.75$ at best odds, $0.66$ at Pinnacle prices), so the relative ordering of settings is fairly stable and the evidence for a strong winner's curse is limited. What does not carry over is the level of CLV: in the holdout it is negative at average and Pinnacle prices (the Pinnacle interval excludes zero) and indistinguishable from zero at best odds, where the development value was positive. The holdout ROI is $+7.5\%$ (95\% CI $[-25.9\%, +43.7\%]$), entirely consistent with the null ($p=0.19$). It comes from 77 bets and sits alongside a negative CLV, so it should not be read as an edge. The sample matters: on the full holdout the same rule returns $+0.3\%$ over 139 bets (Table~\ref{tab:stats_overall}), so the 62 bets on matches without closing odds returned about $-8.6\%$ ($=(139\times0.32\%-77\times7.49\%)/62$). One rule moving from $-8.6\%$ to $+7.5\%$ between two parts of the same holdout is itself a measure of the noise, given standard errors of 13--20 points at these bet counts.

\subsection{The market itself: favourite--longshot bias}
\safetable{market_bias_buckets}
\safetablefit{market_bias_strategies}
\safefigure{results/market_bias.png}{0.95}{Observed versus implied probability and blind ROI by odds bucket.}
This analysis pools $37{,}374$ outcome-level bets, far more than the holdout. The favourite--longshot bias is statistically significant at average odds: the slope of ROI on log-odds is $-0.057$ (95\% CI $[-0.103, -0.009]$, $p=0.022$), meaning each unit increase in log-odds reduces ROI by about 5.7 percentage points. The loss is concentrated in the 6--10 bucket, which returns $-17.4\%$ at average odds (95\% CI $[-26.2\%, -8.7\%]$), $-16.2\%$ at Pinnacle prices and $-6.3\%$ at maximum odds, while favourites ($o<1.5$) are near break-even ($-0.6\%$ at average odds, $0.0\%$ at Pinnacle prices, $+2.2\%$ at maximum odds). The 10+ bucket (938 outcomes) breaks the monotone pattern, with ROI between $-3.4\%$ and $+14.1\%$ depending on price source and intervals tens of points wide, so it is uninformative. The slope is smaller at Pinnacle closing prices ($-0.043$, $p=0.090$) and at maximum odds ($-0.011$, $p=0.684$), but both estimates lie inside the average-odds interval and the slopes were not tested against each other. We therefore do not claim the bias is absent at better prices; maximum odds, being the best across bookmakers, partly offset it by construction.

\subsection{Bankroll risk}
\safetable{risk_drawdown}
\safetablefit{risk_ruin}
\safetable{risk_staking}
\safefigure{results/risk_analysis.png}{0.98}{Equity versus the luck-only band, ruin probability by bankroll, and staking schemes.}
The simulations use the 139 bets of the logistic regression 6\% edge strategy on the full holdout. Under the efficient-market null, a flat 1-unit staking strategy from a 100-unit bankroll yields a median final P\&L of $-11.3$ units (5th percentile: $-39.0$), with a 73.2\% probability of ending negative; the ``observed'' scenario gives a median of $-0.3$ units and a 50.6\% probability of ending negative. Under the null, the probability of ruin is 74.5\% for a 10-unit bankroll and 44.8\% for 20 units, so even ``safe'' flat staking carries substantial risk without an edge. Quarter-Kelly staking with model probabilities is worse: under the null it ends with a median bankroll of 78.9 units against 89.2 for flat staking, and the probability of a drawdown above 50\% is 14.5\% against 2.0\%. A plausible mechanism is that Kelly stakes scale with the estimated edge, and the largest estimated edges are disproportionately estimation noise, so small probability errors are amplified. We did not test this explanation; the logistic regression is well calibrated on average (ECE 0.012), so any miscalibration is local rather than global.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item A single holdout cannot separate a small edge from luck; the power table states what it could detect (22--33\% ROI at the 6\% threshold). More data is the only remedy.
  \item Features exclude lineups, injuries and expected goals, and rest days ignore European and cup fixtures.
  \item Best-odds results assume the best quoted price can actually be obtained; average odds are a pre-closing snapshot, so CLV contains some timing noise.
  \item Closing odds are missing for part of the holdout, so CLV and strategy selection rest on 898 matches; whether those matches are representative (by league or period) was not checked.
  \item In the CLV table the ROI columns of the best-odds rows repeat the average-odds ROI, because profit is computed at average odds for both price sets; only the CLV and pick-skill columns use best odds.
  \item Pick skill should be read against a favourite-backing benchmark, which alone earns about 2 points at average odds.
  \item The favourite--longshot slopes at different price sources were not formally compared, and the 10+ odds bucket is too sparse to interpret.
  \item Power de-vigging is a modelling choice; Shin's method is a natural alternative.
  \item Rules were selected on one season; the holdout evaluates one pre-chosen strategy, but the development grid size and the number of variants tested should be borne in mind.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
No model, blend, or betting strategy beats the de-vigged market with statistical support: all scoring metrics favour the market, the optimised blend weight is $w\approx0$, and every holdout ROI is consistent with the efficient-market null. The CLV analysis reveals a small but statistically significant pick skill ($+2.2\%$) for the logistic regression, but much of it at average odds is what a favourite-leaning rule would give, and it does not overcome the overround: CLV is negative at average and Pinnacle prices. The power analysis shows that samples of this size (139--226 bets at the 6\% threshold, 77 for the selected strategy on the closing-odds subset) cannot distinguish a realistic 2--5\% edge from luck. The risk simulations show that without an edge, flat staking carries a 45--75\% probability of ruin for 10--20 unit bankrolls, and quarter-Kelly staking with model probabilities increases drawdown risk relative to flat staking.
